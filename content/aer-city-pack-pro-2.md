For years, I bought thirty-dollar backpacks from Costco without thinking twice. They carried what I needed, and when they wore out after a year or two, I replaced them. That habit ended abruptly when one of them started causing severe, persistent shoulder pain, even when I was carrying almost nothing. 

Carrying tech gear every day is not just about having a fabric sack with two straps. Between one or two laptops, an iPad, chargers, cables, and daily essentials, the weight adds up quickly. I decided it was time to stop buying disposable bags and invest in a dedicated everyday carry pack with proper ergonomics, durable materials, and smart organization.

## The Search Criteria

My baseline requirements were straightforward. The pack needed enough structured protection for a 16-inch laptop alongside an iPad or secondary machine. It required balanced weight distribution and thick shoulder padding to eliminate shoulder strain. It had to be compact enough for daily commuting without feeling like a turtle shell, yet capable of holding enough clothes and tech for a weekend trip. Finally, a luggage pass-through strap and an external water bottle pocket were non-negotiables for air travel.

I narrowed my research down to four main contenders.

## The Contenders and Deal Breakers

The Almond Oak Expandable Travel Backpack was the budget standout. It had clean aesthetics, solid materials, and an expandable 25L to 30L design. The fatal flaw was its opening mechanism: the clamshell opened sideways like a book rather than top-to-bottom. In daily use, having to swing the entire pack open horizontally just to grab something from the main compartment felt awkward and impractical.

The Evergoods CPL24 V3 is legendary in EDC communities for its contoured harness and bombproof 840D ballistic nylon construction. People swear by how it carries weight. However, Evergoods made two design decisions that disqualified it for me: it lacked an exterior water bottle pocket and had no luggage pass-through strap. For anyone who travels regularly or carries a water bottle daily, those omissions are immediate deal-breakers.

The Aer Travel Pack 3 Small was tempting because it nailed travel functionality. It offered a clamshell opening, dedicated tech storage, compression straps, and luggage integration. But at 28 liters and over three and a half pounds empty, it leaned too far toward travel. Carrying it into a classroom, library, or coffee shop every day felt excessively bulky.

That brought me to the Aer City Pack Pro 2. It hit the exact sweet spot between a structured daily commuter and a streamlined travel bag. Aer offered it in both a 20L and a 24L size. While the 20L is slightly sleeker, the 24L added significant internal capacity with almost no difference in physical height or depth. I pulled the trigger on the 24L in black Cordura ballistic nylon.

## The Details That Stood Out

The build quality was immediately obvious out of the box. The 1680D Cordura fabric and beefy YKK zippers feel virtually indestructible. 

What impressed me most were the thoughtful micro-details. The front admin panel opens with a clean horseshoe zipper, giving quick access to pens, cables, and tech pouches without spilling contents. Aer included dedicated clip points, sturdy side grab handles for pulling the bag out of overhead bins or car seats, and a discreet internal AirTag pocket with a key leash.

Another underrated design detail is that the bag stands completely upright on its own. Most backpacks slump forward onto dirty floors the moment you put them down. The City Pack Pro 2 maintains its shape and stays balanced upright on flat surfaces, as long as it has a reasonable load inside.

## Five Months Later

After five months of daily carry and multiple flights, the shoulder pain has completely vanished. The harness system, padded back panel, and adjustable sternum strap distribute the load evenly across my chest and back. On normal days with just a laptop, charger, and a few accessories, the pack feels remarkably light.

Because the pack has built-in structure, it can look a bit broad depending on your frame. However, the depth naturally compresses down when it is lightly packed, keeping a clean profile. When packed full for travel, it expands to fit several packing cubes while still sliding comfortably under airline seats as a personal item.

My only minor gripes are small. If the pack is completely empty or loaded with an unusual front-heavy balance, it can occasionally tip forward instead of standing upright. Additionally, if I take the pack off awkwardly by one strap, the harness adjustment can occasionally slip a notch and require a quick tug to re-center. 

Aside from those small quirks, the City Pack Pro 2 solved every frustration I had with cheaper backpacks. It proved that paying for thoughtful engineering and proper ergonomics pays dividends every single day.
